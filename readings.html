Reading 1: On Photography by Susan Sontag

I don’t take photos very often, however the most often I have taken photos was for a photography class and when I went on vacation on my own for the first time. From how I interpreted the reading, photography is an art form that many people can capture and keep. In today’s age, you may see many people online talking about consumerism and being materialistic. These characteristics are often encouraged by large companies such as Target or Amazon. They try to produce anything that can be sold based on the most recent online trends. On the other side of the spectrum, people will look down upon consumerism. We are often encouraged to decrease the purchasing of products by our peers online. It’s interesting how in this reading, we are taught that photography can be viewed in a similar manner, as “things” that can be collected and kept as a trophy or as proof of having done something. I do prefer photography that is used as a form of historical documentation, but I’m not opposed to viewing photography as a result of tourism or vacations. When I took my photography class last semester, I took a variety of photos not as historical documentation, but just to take photos of loved ones. On another note, I had a lot of fun taking photos of the things happening around me. I once took photos of a swarm of police officers that stood outside the college for what I could only assume to be some sort of protest or parade. Just like the process of taking those photos, I find it very interesting to listen to photographers speak about their experiences documenting protests. I love to look at photos (and art overall) that can tell a story. Depending on where you look, photography is used for a variety of reasons. Because many people are looking at the news right now and what’s happening around the world right now, we are focusing on photographers that are documenting protests of ICE or conflicts in Palestine. Often, we will see that these photographers get injured in an attempt to capture a good image. I once heard a story of a photographer who threw their camera in the direction of a fellow photographer as they were getting detained by an officer, as an attempt to save the photo they took and prevent the officers from taking the image from them. A story as compelling as that being attached to a simple photo is why documentation of the world around you is far more interesting than a photo of a cool building you saw in my opinion. I hope to take more photos similar to that genre of photography in the future.

I would also like to add that this is no discouragement from taking photos of your loved ones or things that you simply like to look at. At the end of the day, there is value in all genres of photography and art.

Reading 2: Your Brain on Chat GPT

I have been a strong advocate for human-made work and anything human, which in turn means I'm incredibly against the use of AI. While reading both the article and watching the video, the information that an increased use of AI impacts your brain activity in a negative way was not surprising or new information in the slightest. It's unfortunate how often I will come across people who rely so heavily on AI that they are unable to formulate a single original thought. In English class we are taught to think critically and deeply on the reading provided to us, leading to many students questioning why thinking that way is even important in the first place. "Maybe the curtains are just blue". Rather than exercising your brain and practicing critical thinking, some people may much rather spend that time combatting the idea of critical thinking because it's a threat to their comfortable lifestyle. AI feeds the laziness we all crave to feel, and based off the reading, it helps in decreasing brain activity. The interesting thing I noticed when reading through this study was that the researchers conducting said study used ChatGPT to help them perform it. I find this to be contradictory to the results of the study, because if you use AI to research the impacts of AI on the human mind, only to find that there are negative results, then it seems to slightly compromise the results.

When listening to the video featuring Dr. Daniel Amen and Dr. Terry Sejnowski, I learned that the usage of AI is linked to Dementia, which was new but not surprising to me. Just like they state, "If you don't use it, you lose it" which could not be any more true than it already is. When you surrender your brain to ChatGPT and any other AI systems (Claude, Grok, Gemini, etc.), you lose your ability to think for yourself, so you could often find yourself relying on ChatGPT for everything because otherwise you wouldn't be able to do what needs to be done (whether it be complete an assignment, work effectively, or even write an email). Unfortunately, you will see more of this as time goes on with no regulation on the usage of AI. I remember sitting in a class beside a student who had ChatGPT open on her desktop with a variety of conversations involving asking it to write a reply to an email she received. What was interesting for me to learn was that she is a nursing student! On the other hand, the video spends a section of the conversation talking about how to use AI in a manner that can help aid in brain functionality and benefit you as a "long-term learner". In theory, this could be true. However, that would require the entire population of AI users to learn to use it in a way that is beneficial to their brain functionality and evolution. Not only that, but they would also have to be willing to use it in that way, and like I mentioned earlier, AI will feed our craving to be lazy. No one can guarantee that AI users will all use AI in an ethical manner. We have to also consider the environmental effects of AI usage.

Even though the focus of both the paper and the video were the effects AI has on the human brain, it would be ignorant to disregard the effects AI has on the environment. Data Centers have already proven to have detrimental damage to the land surrounding it, the amount of fresh water it uses to function, and most recently discovered (still up for debate as more research has to be done in order to come to a conclusion), the harm it causes to livestock in its vicinity. Many speculate that it harms cattle like causing "slight changes in estrous cycles and milk yield", and there is an idea going around that the EMFs emitting from data centers are causing still born cattle (again, this is purely speculative). Regardless, more research will come out talking on how damaging AI and data centers are to the environment. At the end of the day, I cannot see a reason that AI makes anything better in our world.

Reading 3: Motherhood in Crisis by Laurence Ivil, Alicia Prager and Saidu Bah

A story that interested me would be Kadiatu’s story The Devil at My Door. I always have a soft spot for stories where mothers yearn to be parents but aren’t given the chance due to unfortunate circumstances. It also baffles me quite often how backwards some countries still are when it comes to women’s healthcare and just healthcare in general. When they first spoke about the village’s “traditional healer”, I thought that it would be a woman who actually knew what she was doing. There is a historical significance of midwives in Africa and may have in fact originated there. Midwifery made bigger waves when enslaved African women were taken to America. They were made a crucial role for many births among enslaved people as well as the slave owner’s wives. I was surprised to find out that the “traditional healer” was not a woman, but a man who blamed Kadiatu for her children’s deaths. I noticed that the story stated she lost two children at birth in 2007. I don’t know if they died because they were stillborn, or if she miscarried. There are a few studies that show that miscarriage rates are connected to male genetics. In particular, the quality of male sperm. If she had miscarried the children, there is a chance it wasn’t her fault in the slightest, but her husbands instead.

One reasoning as to why illustration could’ve been used to tell this story (along with all the others), is to engage the reader more. Illustration was probably used so that we can connect with and empathize with Kadiatu’s story. Being shown imagery makes us feel more involved in the story, and we can see what she is going through. Another consideration would be that many people may be more enthused by the idea of reading a comic book style story compared to just reading an article. The illustrations vary from photographs mainly by the colors of the art. Bold line art and simplified colors majorly contribute to our ability to differentiate between the photographs and the illustrations. At the same time, the style of the illustrations are the best fitted for the content of the story. It wouldn't be appropriate if the artist of these illustrations chose to draw in a more cartoonish style similar to a children’s tv show. That would dull the impact of the story to its viewers and readers. I also find the interviews to be a nice cherry on top for the stories. We get to listen to people who know best about the situation happening in Sierra Leone, and the people who have helped these moms as well as other people in similar situations. If this story was only told through photographs, and no words/explanations, we would have a lot of difficulty deciphering what is happening in the story.
